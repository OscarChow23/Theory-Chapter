\section{Experimental Landscape}
\label{sec:landscape}

% Parameter provenance -> current constraints -> open questions -> future reach.
% Citation convention: page numbers refer to the arXiv version named in each bib
% entry, not to journal pagination. Sources cited by table or figure number use
% pagination-independent identifiers. See the header comment in main.bib.

The preceding sections developed the three-flavour oscillation framework and identified
the spectral features that carry parameter information. This section asks where the
numbers in that framework actually come from. The six oscillation parameters are not
measured by any single experiment; each is constrained by a distinct class of
measurement, characterised by the baseline-to-energy ratio it samples and by the
flavour it can tag. Reading the parameter set in this way makes clear both why the
present picture is as precise as it is, and why three questions --- the mass ordering,
the $\theta_{23}$ octant and the value of $\dcp$ --- remain
open.\footnote{Page numbers accompanying a citation in this section refer to the arXiv
version identified in the corresponding bibliography entry, so that every quoted value
can be located in a freely accessible document of fixed pagination.}

% ============================================================
\subsection{Solar, Atmospheric and Reactor Measurements}
\label{sec:landscape-natural}

\paragraph{The solar sector.}
The parameters $\theta_{12}$ and $\dm{21}$ were first probed by the radiochemical
solar-neutrino experiments --- Homestake~\cite{Davis1968}, followed by
SAGE~\cite{Abazov1991} and GALLEX~\cite{Anselmann1992} --- and by the water-Cherenkov
measurement of Kamiokande-II~\cite{Hirata1990}. These experiments established a deficit
relative to the solar model, but could not by themselves distinguish flavour conversion
from an incorrect flux prediction. That ambiguity was closed by SNO's neutral-current
measurement~\cite{Ahmad2002}, which showed the total active flux to be consistent with
prediction while the $\nue$ component was suppressed.

The solar sector is distinctive in that the suppression is not a vacuum oscillation
effect. Neutrinos produced in the solar core traverse a steeply falling electron
density and undergo the adiabatic conversion described in Sec.~\ref{sec:matter}. Because
that conversion is resonant only for one sign of $\dm{21}$, the solar data fix not only
the magnitude of $\dm{21}$ but also its sign, and resolve the octant of $\theta_{12}$ ---
the only mixing angle whose octant is currently known. This is the reason the solar
splitting is conventionally written as a positive quantity while the atmospheric
splitting retains an undetermined sign.

Reactor antineutrinos provide the complementary vacuum measurement. KamLAND, observing
reactors at an average baseline of order $180$\,km, saw the spectral distortion
characteristic of $\dm{21}$ and confirmed the solar interpretation in a
matter-effect-free environment~\cite{KamLAND2013}.

\paragraph{JUNO and the solar sector today.}
That picture changed substantially with the first data from JUNO. Using a $20$\,kt
liquid-scintillator detector at a baseline of about $52$\,km --- chosen so that the
$\dm{21}$ oscillation reaches its first minimum within the reactor flux --- JUNO
reported a simultaneous determination of both solar parameters from a single data set
of $59.1$ days~\citep[p.~15]{JUNO2026}:
\begin{equation}
    \sin^{2}\theta_{12} = 0.3092 \pm 0.0087,
    \qquad
    \dm{21} = (7.50 \pm 0.12)\times 10^{-5}~\text{eV}^{2},
    \label{eq:juno-solar}
\end{equation}
improving the precision on both parameters by a factor of $1.6$ relative to all previous
measurements combined, and reducing the uncertainty on $\dm{21}$ to
$1.55\%$~\citep[p.~15]{JUNO2026}. Figure~\ref{fig:juno-solar} shows the resulting
confidence intervals alongside the earlier KamLAND and solar determinations.

It is worth being precise about what this measurement is. JUNO's first result constrains
the \emph{solar} pair; the atmospheric splitting $\dm{31}$ and the reactor angle
$\theta_{13}$ enter the fit as external constraints~\citep[p.~15]{JUNO2026} rather than
as outputs. Resolving the fast $\dm{31}$ oscillation imprinted on the slower solar
modulation --- from which JUNO's mass-ordering sensitivity derives --- requires far
greater exposure.

\paragraph{The atmospheric sector.}
The parameters $\theta_{23}$ and $\dm{32}$ were first established by Super-Kamiokande's
observation of a zenith-angle-dependent deficit in atmospheric
$\numu$~\cite{Fukuda1998}. Atmospheric neutrinos span several decades in $L/E$, and so
sample many oscillation cycles rather than the single cycle available to a fixed
baseline; the trade-off is a broad, poorly constrained energy resolution. Modern
atmospheric measurements from IceCube DeepCore~\cite{IceCube2023} now reach a precision
on $\dm{32}$ comparable to accelerator experiments, and their allowed regions appear
alongside the accelerator constraints in Fig.~\ref{fig:nova-contour}.

\paragraph{The reactor angle.}
Short-baseline reactor experiments measure $\theta_{13}$ through $\nuebar$ disappearance
at $L \sim 1$--$2$\,km, where the $\dm{31}$ oscillation is near its first minimum and the
solar term is negligible. Daya Bay's final data set gives the most precise determination,
$\sin^{2}2\theta_{13} = 0.0851 \pm 0.0024$~\cite{DayaBay2023}. Its importance here is
indirect but decisive: measured in a channel with no $\dcp$ dependence and negligible
matter effect, $\theta_{13}$ can be imported into long-baseline appearance fits as an
external constraint. Without it the appearance rate would be
a function of two unknowns at once, and neither $\dcp$ nor the ordering could be
extracted from it --- which is why the long-baseline results below are quoted both with
and without it applied.

\subsection{The Wider Programme}
\label{sec:landscape-wider}

% IceCube DeepCore and, in the Mediterranean, KM3NeT/ORCA instrument very large volumes of

% \paragraph{Hyper-Kamiokande.}
% Hyper-Kamiokande is the successor to Super-Kamiokande, a water-Cherenkov detector of

% exposed to an upgraded J-PARC beam on T2K's $295$\,km baseline. Its strategy is the
