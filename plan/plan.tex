\section{Chapter goal and scope}

Write the neutrino-theory background chapter of the PhD thesis: standard theory of neutrino
oscillations, developed enough to support the physics arguments used in later analysis chapters.

\paragraph{Distinctive angle.} The chapter follows a spectrum-first argument tailored to NOvA:
the historical and Standard Model setup leads into oscillation theory, which is then used to
interpret the far-detector energy spectrum and explain the experiment's parameter sensitivity. Two
things are foregrounded that most surveyed theses treat only in passing --- now developed together
in one section (\S\ref{plan:spectrum}):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Spectral features} --- taking the full 3-flavour probability equations and
    showing, term by term, which features in the observed far-detector energy spectrum correspond
    to which physics inputs. This is the pedagogical discussion that most existing theses skip.
    \item \textbf{Disappearance-led sensitivity arguments} --- why the $\nu_\mu$ disappearance
    spectrum constrains $\Delta m^{2}_{32}$ and $\theta_{23}$, matching the emphasis of this
    thesis. The $\nu_e$ appearance channel, $\delta_{\rm CP}$, mass ordering and their degeneracies
    remain part of the complete three-flavour picture, but receive less space.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Boundaries.} NOvA hardware, beam construction, and detector-specific numbers stay in
the NOvA chapter that follows. Physics-level motivation for the NOvA regime ($\sim 810$~km
baseline, $\sim 2$~GeV peak energy) is fair game here as a theory-motivated design argument.
Numerical results from the analyses stay in their own chapters.

\paragraph{Length target.} Aim for the lower-middle of a flexible $\sim 21$--$28$ page range:
history $\sim 2$~pp; Standard Model $\sim 2$--$4$~pp; mixing and oscillations
$\sim 9$--$11$~pp; spectrum and sensitivity $\sim 5$--$8$~pp; experimental landscape
$\sim 3$~pp. These are writing guides rather than quotas.

\paragraph{Deadline.} First draft by 2026-09-01.

\subsection{A Brief History of the Neutrino}
\label{plan:history}

\textbf{File:} \texttt{sections/01\_history.tex}

\textbf{Purpose:} Ease the reader in with the story of how neutrinos went from a bookkeeping
postulate to a directly-detected particle to an evidence-of-new-physics probe.

\textbf{Content:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pauli's postulate (1930) to explain the continuous $\beta$-decay spectrum
    \item Cowan--Reines direct detection (1956), retaining the delayed-coincidence principle
    while trimming detector minutiae
    \item Discovery of $\nu_\mu$ (Lederman--Schwartz--Steinberger, 1962) and $\nu_\tau$ (DONUT, 2000)
    \item Pontecorvo / MNS flavour-mixing proposals; solar and atmospheric anomalies
    $\to$ Super-K (1998) and SNO (2002) oscillation evidence
    \item Closing bridge written by Oscar: flavour change requires mass and mixing, in tension
    with the massless neutrinos of the Standard Model; leave chirality and couplings for the next
    section
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Models from survey:} Sweeney \S1.2 (Genesis / Observing / Oscillations); Martinez \S1.1
(discovery narrative); Sep\'ulveda Ch.~1 (broader intro chapter).

\textbf{Length target:} $\sim 2$~pp. The current draft is close to target; revisions should
improve focus and the hand-off rather than add another historical episode.

\subsection{Neutrinos in the Standard Model}
\label{plan:sm}

\textbf{File:} \texttt{sections/02\_standard\_model.tex}

\textbf{Purpose:} Set up the framework and highlight why non-zero neutrino mass is surprising ---
motivates everything else in the chapter.

\textbf{Planned subsections:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The Weak Interaction and the Meaning of Flavour
    \item Chirality and the Absence of Neutrino Mass
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Content:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Open by answering the tension raised at the end of the history section: SM fermion
    content and gauge structure, including only left-handed neutrinos
    \item Weak charged and neutral currents at the level of the CC vertex current only: V--A
    structure, the left-chiral projector, NC flavour universality, and flavour tagging via the
    charged lepton in CC interactions
    \item Explain why the minimal Standard Model has no renormalisable Dirac neutrino mass term,
    then land the punchline: the SM says $m_\nu = 0$, while oscillations require otherwise
    \item Do not develop the see-saw mechanism or a Dirac--Majorana comparison. At most note that
    mass generation lies beyond the minimal SM and that Majorana phases do not affect oscillations.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Note on interactions (2026-07-17):} fundamental CC/NC couplings belong here. Detailed
QE/RES/DIS, nuclear effects (RFG, MEC, FSI) and cross-section systematics stay in the later
NOvA simulation / interaction chapter --- they concern reconstruction and systematics, not the
electroweak setup of oscillations.

\textbf{Models from survey:} Booth \S2.2.1 and Veera \S1.2 bracket the depth wanted here, and
this section sits between them; Sweeney \S1.1 (clean SM-first framing); Sep\'ulveda \S2.1
(compact SM). Follow none of them structurally --- see the 2026-07-27 decisions.

\textbf{Length target:} $\sim 1.5$--$2$~pp. Do not reintroduce the electroweak field-theory
machinery, mass models or interaction-mode digressions.

\subsection{Neutrino Mixing and Oscillations}
\label{plan:oscillations}

\textbf{File:} \texttt{sections/03\_oscillations.tex}

\textbf{Purpose:} Develop the full oscillation framework in one continuous argument: mixing
$\to$ two-flavour derivation $\to$ three-flavour probabilities $\to$ matter effects.

\textbf{Planned subsections:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Flavour and Mass Eigenstates
    \item The PMNS Matrix
    \item Oscillation Parameters and Mass Ordering
    \item Two-Flavour Oscillations
    \item Three-Flavour Oscillations
    \item Matter Effects
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Content --- mixing / PMNS:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Flavour vs.\ mass eigenstates; the mixing matrix $U_{\rm PMNS}$
    \item Standard parameterisation as product of three rotations $\times$ a CP phase
    \item Table of current best-fit values (PDG or NuFIT), normal vs.\ inverted ordering
    \item Comment on Majorana phases (irrelevant for oscillations $\to$ set aside)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Content --- two-flavour:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Mass eigenstates propagate as plane waves; flavour eigenstates as superpositions
    \item Time evolution $\to$ interference $\to$
    \[
        P(\nu_\alpha \to \nu_\beta) = \sin^{2}(2\theta) \sin^{2}\!\left(
            \tfrac{\Delta m^{2}\,L}{4E}
        \right)
    \]
    \item Oscillation length $L_{\rm osc} = 4\pi E / \Delta m^{2}$; where the first maximum sits
    \item $L/E$ as the natural experimental variable; where the ``dip'' lives
    \item \textbf{Wave-packet caveat} --- one sentence + footnote: the plane-wave derivation
    glosses over that mass eigenstates have different dispersion relations and would in principle
    decohere at a wave-packet-dependent coherence length. For NOvA (and every current oscillation
    experiment) the coherence length vastly exceeds $L$, so the plane-wave answer is correct.
    Cite Kayser~(1981), Giunti--Kim textbook, Beuthe review.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Content --- three-flavour:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item General 3-flavour form; the two independent $\Delta m^{2}_{ij}$
    \item Full annotated expansion of $P(\nu_\mu \to \nu_\mu)$, foregrounding the dependence on
    $\theta_{23}$ and $\Delta m^{2}_{32}$ that is most relevant to this thesis
    \item Flag that the leading disappearance dependence on $\sin^{2}(2\theta_{23})$ is
    octant-degenerate; reserve the role of appearance and higher-order terms in breaking that
    degeneracy for Sec.~\ref{plan:spectrum}
    \item A real but shorter expansion of $P(\nu_\mu \to \nu_e)$: identify the leading atmospheric,
    solar and $\delta_{\rm CP}$-carrying interference terms without matching the disappearance
    treatment in length
    \item Annotate the physical dependence and approximate size of the retained terms
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Content --- matter effects (MSW):}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Effective potential $V_{\rm CC} = \sqrt{2}\,G_{F} n_{e}$ from $\nu_e$--$e^-$ coherent
    forward CC scattering
    \item NC potential from Z exchange acts equally on all three flavours $\Rightarrow$ cancels
    out of oscillation probabilities
    \item Modified Hamiltonian in flavour basis $\Rightarrow$ constant-density / slab result
    stated with physical intuition, not derived in full here
    \item \textbf{Explicit note on $\nu_\mu$ disappearance:} the leading 2-flavour
    $\nu_\mu \leftrightarrow \nu_\tau$ channel is unaffected by matter; 3-flavour corrections
    are $\sim$\% level for NOvA
    \item \textbf{Appearance carries the visible matter sensitivity:} matter modifies the
    effective $\theta_{13}$ with opposite sign for $\nu$ vs $\bar\nu$; the short derivation and
    consequences are deferred to Sec.~\ref{plan:spectrum}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Models:} Sep\'ulveda \S2.2 / \S2.2.1 / \S2.2.4 / \S2.3; Booth \S2.3 / \S2.3.3 / \S2.4;
Sweeney \S1.3 / \S1.3.2; Martinez \S1.3.

\textbf{Length target:} $\sim 9$--$11$~pp total. If this section grows, compress the matter
treatment before sacrificing the two-flavour derivation or disappearance-led three-flavour
discussion.

\subsection{Spectrum and Sensitivity}
\label{plan:spectrum}

\textbf{File:} \texttt{sections/04\_spectrum\_and\_sensitivity.tex}

\textbf{Purpose:} Translate the oscillation equations into what NOvA sees in the FD spectrum,
then answer why those features give sensitivity to particular parameters. The disappearance
spectrum is the analytical centre of gravity, reflecting the emphasis of this thesis. The
appearance channel completes the three-flavour picture but is deliberately shorter. This remains
a physics-level argument with no NOvA hardware.

\textbf{Planned subsections:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Probability versus $L/E$
    \item The NOvA Oscillation Window
    \item The Disappearance Spectrum
    \item The Appearance Spectrum
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Content --- spectral features:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Global picture --- brief.} Sketch $P(\nu_\mu\!\to\!\nu_\mu)$ vs $L/E$ over
    many decades. Fast atmospheric wiggle ($\Delta m^{2}_{32}$) and slow solar envelope
    ($\Delta m^{2}_{21}$). One figure, one paragraph.
    \item \textbf{Zoom to NOvA's window.} At fixed $L=810$~km and $E \sim 1$--$3$~GeV, one
    atmospheric oscillation cycle, not multiple wiggles.
    \item \textbf{Disappearance.} Single broad dip: position fixes $\Delta m^{2}_{32}$; depth
    fixes $\sin^{2}(2\theta_{23})$. Why the dip region ($L/E \approx L_{\rm osc}/2$) gives the
    sharpest $\Delta m^{2}_{32}$ handle. This is the longest subsection.
    \item Explain the leading $\theta_{23}$ octant degeneracy and briefly identify how appearance
    information and sub-leading effects help resolve it.
    \item \textbf{Appearance.} Single broad peak: rate carries $\theta_{13}$--$\delta_{\rm CP}$
    interference and (via matter) mass ordering; shape carries less information. Keep this
    materially shorter than the disappearance discussion. Fold CP, mass ordering and their
    degeneracies into this subsection rather than giving them a separate heading.
    \item \textbf{Neutrino vs.\ antineutrino running.} What changes in each equation and spectrum.
    \item Figures: (a) global $P(\nu_\mu\to\nu_\mu)$ vs $L/E$; (b) NOvA window zoom with the
    first oscillation maximum marked; (c) oscillated vs.\ unoscillated FD disappearance spectrum,
    annotated with dip position $\leftrightarrow \Delta m^{2}_{32}$ and depth
    $\leftrightarrow \sin^{2}(2\theta_{23})$. Appearance may receive one small panel only if it
    earns the space.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Content --- sensitivity / degeneracies (within appearance):}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Appearance vs.\ disappearance probe orthogonal combinations of parameters
    \item $\delta_{\rm CP}$ appears only in appearance --- $\nu_{e}$ vs $\bar\nu_{e}$ rate
    difference is the primary handle
    \item \textbf{Small derivation --- matter-modified appearance probability.}
    From the vacuum $P(\nu_\mu \to \nu_e)$ expansion, introduce the MSW potential and show how
    the leading atmospheric term picks up an effective $\theta_{13}$ shifted by
    $\pm 2 E V_{\rm CC} / \Delta m^{2}_{31}$. Sign flips for $\bar\nu$ (or when the ordering
    flips). A short passage of algebra following Nunokawa--Parke--Valle; not a full
    three-eigenvalue diagonalisation.
    \item Consequence: $\nu$/$\bar\nu$ asymmetry combines matter + $\delta_{\rm CP}$
    degenerately; a long baseline separates them because matter scales differently with $E$.
    \item Physics motivation for $L \sim 810$~km, $E \sim 2$~GeV: oscillation maximum for
    $\Delta m^{2}_{32}$ \emph{and} matter effect large enough to help break the
    ordering--$\delta_{\rm CP}$ degeneracy. \emph{Numbers only; hardware later.}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Reframing note (2026-06-30):} NOvA scans a narrow $L/E$ window and sees a single
oscillation cycle, not multiple wiggles. Global picture kept briefly as context.

\textbf{Models:} Booth \S2.4; Sep\'ulveda \S2.5. None of the surveyed theses develops the
spectral features end-to-end at NOvA's operating regime.

\textbf{Length target:} $\sim 5$--$8$~pp. Appearance, including CP/ordering/degeneracies,
occupies $\sim 1.5$--$2.5$~pp.

    \item Solar $\to$ $\theta_{12}, \Delta m^{2}_{21}$ (with matter-effect-driven resolution of

    \item \textbf{Updated 2026-08-05:} JUNO's June~2026 update (207~days; Neutrino/PASCOS)

    \item Long-baseline appearance $\to$ $\delta_{\rm CP}$ and mass ordering (still open)
    \item Latest NOvA 3-flavour results, briefly: prioritise disappearance-relevant best fits for

    \item A fair NOvA--T2K comparison, concentrating on where their constraints or preferred

    \item \textbf{Revised 2026-08-05:} Next-Generation Experiments covers DUNE and Hyper-K

\textbf{Conventions fixed 2026-08-03:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Headline numbers come only from journal or arXiv sources, never from conference
    slides. Page numbers cited with \verb|\citep[p.~N]{...}| refer to the arXiv version
    named in the \texttt{version} field of the bib entry, not journal pagination.
    \item Future sensitivities are quoted against exposure (kt$\cdot$MW$\cdot$yr, POT),

    the global fit.
\end{itemize}

\section{Legacy Masters-Thesis Reference}

\noindent\textbf{Status:} The material below is retained as a legacy reference only. It does not
define the structure, scope or wording of the planned theory chapter.

\noindent Ever since its proposal by Wolfgang Pauli in 1930, the elusive neutrino has been and remains a subject of intrigue for particle physicists. In the SM, neutrinos belong to the lepton family and come in three flavours, each corresponding to their charged lepton counterparts, the electron neutrino ($\nu_e$), muon neutrino ($\nu_{\mu}$) and tau neutrino ($\nu_{\tau}$). Neutrinos are electrically neutral, do not carry colour charge and are massless in the SM, hence they only interact via the weak force. They are produced in vast quantities through many processes and as such, are ubiquitous in the universe. The primary neutrino sources include supernovae, cosmic ray and stellar processes. On Earth they can be produced in high energy particle accelerator experiments and are generated as a byproduct of beta decay processes in fission reactors. Multiple groundbreaking discoveries have been made in the near century of them being studied and theorised. One such discovery is the understanding that neutrinos do in fact have mass, contrary to what is predicted by the SM. The observed phenomenon of neutrino oscillations requires these particles to have mass. 

\vspace{10pt}

In this chapter, neutrinos and their discovery will be presented. The physics of the particle including the aforementioned phenomenon of neutrino oscillations will be also introduced and finally, an overview of their interactions with matter allowing for their detection will be covered. 


\subsection{Neutrinos in the Standard Model}
\setcounter{equation}{0}
\noindent The nuclear process of beta decay, 

\begin{equation}
    n \xrightarrow{} \Bar{\nu}_e + e^- + p,
\end{equation}
which was first observed in the early 20\textsuperscript{th} century, was first accompanied by a challenge known as the continuous spectrum problem. This issue concerned observations of the energy spectra of the beta particle (high energy $e^-$) emitted in this process. The energy spectra of the electrons did not align with the initially predicted discrete spectrum \cite{rutherford}. It was expected that the electron released from the beta decay should have an energy distribution similar to the discrete energy levels observed in atomic absorption and emission spectra. However, it was measured that the electron could have a continuous range of energies As a result, it was proposed that a new, neutral and hence undetectable particle should be considered and introduced. This was first seen as a quick solution to this problem and accounted for the missing energy/momentum that initially posed confusion. It was Wolfgang Pauli who proposed the existence of this new particle in 1930, which was named the neutrino. Inspired by this new proposition, Enrico Fermi postulated an effective theory, known as Fermi Theory, which describes a 4 point interaction which included this new particle, the neutrino \cite{Gavin}. This theory was the first introduction of the weak nuclear force in the SM. Throughout the years, the weak force was refined so as to neatly fit into the SM. Neutrinos fit quite well in this description as neutral massless particles and come with their respective antiparticles. 

\vspace{10pt}

In the SM, neutrinos are described to be massless, and like the photon, they should not experience time. Therefore, a neutrino produced as a given flavour state at one given point in spacetime should remain in that same flavour state as it propagates. However, this massless nature of the neutrino was put into question and challenged after some observations were made. These observations contradicted the so-called Standard Solar Model (SSM) at the time, leading to the so-called solar neutrino problem. In the Sun, fusion processes occur, for example one where two hydrogen nuclei (protons) collide and fuse together in a high energy collision producing a helium nucleus, an electron neutrino, a positron and some energy corresponding to the difference in rest mass energies of the two protons and the fusion products. This is the principal process by which the Sun produces its energy. Through calculations and the SSM, the electron neutrino flux was predicted. However, when the Homestake Experiment \cite{Homestake} measured this flux with its Chlorine-Argon detector in 1968 \cite{Davis}, the result was that there were fewer chlorine-argon conversions than predicted by the SSM, where each such conversion corresponded to interactions of solar neutrinos with the detector. Later, experiments such as Kamiokande (1986) \cite{Hirata}, a water Cherenkov detector and GALLEX (1992) and SAGE (1991), two Gallium tank based neutrino experiments \cite{Anselmann} \cite{abazov}, all confirmed this solar neutrino deficit. One of the prevailing ideas put forward to explain this was the concept oscillation of electron neutrinos to muon or tau neutrinos. Namely, the electron neutrinos would transform into different flavour neutrinos. These oscillations would occur as a result of the time taken for the neutrinos to travel from the core of the Sun until the point at which they interact with the detector. This hypothesis of neutrino oscillations was very promising but needed to be measured. The following section will explore the mathematics and theoretical aspects of neutrino oscillations. 

 \subsection{Neutrino Oscillations}
 \setcounter{equation}{0}
\noindent The phenomenon of neutrino oscillations is defined by the non-zero probability for a neutrino produced in one flavour, to oscillate to another flavour, when measured at two different spacetime points. To understand how this phenomenon works, two sets of neutrino eigenstates need to be introduced. First, there are the neutrino mass eigenstates, $\ket{\nu_i}$, where the indices $i=1,2,3$ correspond to the three neutrino mass states. Next, there also exists the neutrino flavour eigenstates $\nu_{\alpha}$, with $\alpha = e, \mu, \tau$ each describing the flavour of the neutrinos as they are produced and detected. These form two coupled sets of orthogonal neutrino states, where the flavour eigenstates can be written as a linear superposition of the mass eigenstates, and vice-versa. $\nu_i$ are the stationary states of the free particle Hamiltonian and correspond to particles with definite mass. These evolve according to the Time-Dependent Schrödinger equation with plane wave solutions of the form:
\begin{equation} 
    \ket{\nu_i(t)} = e^{-iE_it}\ket{\nu_i(t=0)} = e^{-iE_it}\ket{\nu_i},
\end{equation}
where $E_i = \sqrt{p^2 + m_i^2}$. In contrast to $\nu_{\alpha}$, these states have a definite mass, suggesting that $\nu_{\alpha}$ should not evolve with time. Since these two mass and flavour eigenbases are orthogonal, they can be related by a 3x3 mixing matrix $U_{PMNS}$.
\begin{equation}
    \begin{pmatrix} \nu_e \\ \nu_{\mu} \\ \nu_{\tau} \end{pmatrix} = U_{PMNS} \begin{pmatrix}
        \nu_1 \\ \nu_2 \\ \nu_3 
    \end{pmatrix} =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        U_{e1} & U_{e2} & U_{e3} \\ U_{\mu 1} & U_{\mu 2} & U_{\mu 3} \\ U_{\tau 1} & U_{\tau 2} & U_{\tau 3}
    \end{pmatrix}
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \nu_1 \\ \nu_2 \\ \nu_3 
    \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
This mixing matrix, known as the Pontecorvo-Maki-Nakagawa-Sakata (PMNS) matrix, satisfies the unitarity condition $ UU^{\dagger} = \mathds{1} $ and can be expressed in terms of three real parameters corresponding to three dimensional rotation angles $\theta_1$, $\theta_2$ and $\theta_3$, and one complex phase factor, $\delta$ \cite{Thomson}. $U_{PMNS}$ can be parameterised into the product of 3 matrices corresponding to different neutrino sources:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{pmatrix}
        U_{e1} & U_{e2} & U_{e3} \\ U_{\mu 1} & U_{\mu 2} & U_{\mu 3} \\ U_{\tau 1} & U_{\tau 2} & U_{\tau 3}
    \end{pmatrix}
    = \begin{pmatrix}
        1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & c_{23} & s_{23} \\ 0 & -s_{23} & c_{23}
    \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
    c_{13} & 0 & s_{13}e^{-i\delta} \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -s_{13}e^{i \delta} & 0 & c_{13}
\end{pmatrix}    
\begin{pmatrix}
    c_{12} & s_{12} & 0 \\ -s_{12} & c_{12} & 0  \\ 0& 0& 1
\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
where $s_{ij} = \sin\theta_{ij}$ and $c_{ij} = \cos\theta_{ij}$. 


To illustrate how neutrino oscillations occur, let us expand (2.2.2), where $\ket{\nu_{\alpha}}$ is neutrino flavour eigenstate as it is produced, at time $t=0$:
\begin{equation}
    \ket{\nu_{\alpha}} = U_{\alpha1}\ket{\nu_1} + U_{\alpha2}\ket{\nu_2} + U_{\alpha3}\ket{\nu_3}.
\end{equation}
We see therefore that flavour eigenstates have components (with different amplitudes) of each different mass eigenstate. The time dependent nature of $\ket{\nu_i}$ means that at a later time $t$, the $\ket{\nu_i}$ will have have propagated as plane waves according to (2.2.1), resulting in a new flavour state:
\begin{equation}
    \ket{\nu_{\alpha}(t)} = U_{\alpha1}e^{-i\phi_1}\ket{\nu_1}+ U_{\alpha2}e^{-i\phi_2}\ket{\nu_2} + U_{\alpha3}e^{-i\phi_3}\ket{\nu_3},
\end{equation}
where $\phi_i = p_i \cdot x_i = (E_it - \mathbf{p_i} \cdot \mathbf{x}).$ $\ket{\nu_{\alpha}(t)}$ can clearly be $\neq \ket{\nu_{\alpha}}$, this suggests that the flavour states can be different after propagation. This is the essence of neutrino oscillations. 

To further emphasise this idea, it is useful to consider how one might obtain oscillation probabilities ($P(\nu_{\alpha}\xrightarrow{}\nu_{\beta})$, where $\nu_{\alpha}$ and $\nu_{\beta}$ are different neutrino flavours). A non-zero value for this implies neutrino oscillations. Using the example of a neutrino beam oscillation experiment, where the goal is to study the number of oscillated neutrinos from one (near) detector compared to another (far) detector, for a primarily $\nu_{\alpha}$ beam, studying oscillations consists of evaluating $\nu_{\beta}$ appearance and $\nu_{\alpha}$ disappearance at a far detector. The appearance and disappearance probabilities can be calculated in terms of these PMNS matrix elements using the above linearly combined expressions. 

\begin{equation}
    P(\nu_{\alpha}\xrightarrow{}\nu_{\beta}) = |\bra{\nu_{\beta}(t)\ket{\nu_{\alpha}}}^2.
\end{equation}

These probabilities are necessary to obtain important measurements. Full derivations of these oscillation probabilities can be found \cite{suekane, Cremonesi, Derek, Thomson}, but they will be stated below:

\begin{equation}
    P(\nu_{\alpha} \xrightarrow{}\nu_{\beta}) = \sum_{i,j=1}^3 U_{\alpha i}^*U_{\beta i}U_{\alpha j}U_{\beta j}^*e^{-i\dfrac{\Delta m^2_{ij}}{2E}t},
\end{equation}
where $\Delta m^2_{ij} = m_j^2 - m_i^2$ is the mass difference between neutrino mass eigenstates. The assumption that neutrinos are highly relativistic ($E\approx |p|$) was made here. It is clear therefore, that neutrino oscillations are in fact a result of a non-zero value of this probability. Expanded, this probability for neutrino oscillations in vacuum can be expressed as \cite{Derek}:

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
& P(\nu_{\alpha} \xrightarrow{}\nu_{\beta}) = \delta_{\alpha \beta} - 4 \sum_{i<j} Re[U_{\alpha i}U_{\beta i}^*U_{\alpha j}^*U_{\beta j}]\sin^2\bigg(\frac{\Delta m^2_{ij}L}{4E}\bigg) \\
&\phantom{+} \phantom{+} + 2 \sum_{i<j}Im[U_{\alpha i}U_{\beta i}^*U_{\alpha j}^*U_{\beta j}]\sin\bigg(\frac{\Delta m^2_{ij}L}{4E}\bigg),
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where $\delta_{\alpha \beta}$ is the Kronecker delta. This expression shows that for massless neutrinos, the probability of oscillation ($\alpha \neq \beta$) is zero and hence the survival probability ($\alpha = \beta$) is unity (no oscillations).

The NOvA experiment uses a $\nu_{\mu}$ beam, so the oscillation probabilities of interest are the $\nu_e$ appearance and $\nu_{\mu}$ survival probabilities. Working through, (2.2.7)/(2.2.8) \cite{Thomson} gives the following expressions for these:

\begin{equation}
    P(\nu_{\mu} \xrightarrow{} \nu_e) \approx \sin^22\theta_{13} \sin^2\theta_{23}\sin^2\bigg(1.27 \cdot \frac{\Delta m^2_{32} L}{E_{\nu}}\bigg),
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    P(\nu_{\mu} \xrightarrow{} \nu_{\mu}) \approx 1 - \cos^4\theta_{13} \sin^22\theta_{23}\sin^2\bigg(1.27 \cdot \frac{\Delta m^2_{32} L}{E_{\nu}}\bigg),
\end{equation}

where the factor of $1.27$ is a result of $1/\hbar c$ coming from the km to $GeV$ unit conversion, $L$ is the distance travelled by the neutrinos in $km$ and $E_{\nu}$ is the neutrino energy. We can see then, that long-baseline experiments like NOvA are sensitive to some of these important neutrino oscillation parameters. %These crucial parameters underlie some of the most critical open questions in neutrino physics, which will be discussed in the following section.

\subsection{Neutrino Interactions}
As will be made evident throughout this report, one of the principal aims of this project is to investigate neutrino-nucleus interactions in the NOvA ND. To continue with an understanding and appreciation of these interactions and how we detect them, an introduction to neutrino interactions is crucial.

As previously states, neutrinos only interact via the weak and gravitational forces. Their interaction probability is so low that it would take light year's thickness worth of lead for a neutrino\footnote{This is only true for low energy neutrinos $\mathcal{O}$(GeV), and not for Ultra High Energy neutrinos $\mathcal{O}$(PeV) \cite{madsen}.} to have a 50\% chance of being stopped (interact). This means detecting these in our detector is very difficult and can only be done by measuring its final state charged particles that produce tracks in the detector.

\vspace{10pt}

Electroweak unification of the electromagnetic and the weak forces is a fundamental theory of particle physics. Out of this theory emerges the electroweak massive gauge bosons ($Z^0$ and $W^{\pm}$) and the massless photon $\gamma$. The $Z^0$ and $W^{\pm}$ massive boson fields mediate weak interactions and couple to the neutrino fields. This is the only SM force by which neutrinos interact, so these bosons are therefore integral to all neutrino interactions with matter. Neutrino interactions that exchange charge through the $W^{\pm}$ boson propagators are named charged-current. Figure 2.1 (a) shows that there is flavour conservation at the neutrino-$W$-charged lepton vertex. As a result, in these interactions, a charged lepton of the same flavour as the incoming neutrino is produced, so that by detecting this charged lepton, the neutrino flavour can be determined\footnote{Practically, due to the short lifetime and decay topologies of the $\tau$ lepton, detecting $\nu_{\tau}$ interactions is very difficult \cite{tau}.}. Conversely, neutral-current (NC) interactions involve the exchange of the neutral $Z^0$ boson. These is no charge exchange here and from Figure 2.1 (b), it is evident that the incoming neutrino flavour cannot be determined, since the only detected particles would be those arising from the hadronic system due to the neutrino's collision with the target nucleus. Due to them interacting almost exclusively via the weak force, the only way of detecting and identifying them is if the charged particles in the final state of CC interactions with the nucleons of the nuclei of the detector material. Hence, to gain insight into the behaviour of this illusive particle, studying and understanding neutrino-nucleon interactions is critical.

\subsection{Neutrino-Nucleon Interactions} \label{sec:nu-int}

\noindent The weak boson fields, which generate the gauge bosons that mediate these neutrino-nucleus interactions are coupled with both neutrino and quark fields and allow these interactions to occur. Similarly to scattering of other particles, depending on the energy of the incoming neutrino, the interaction might look different. For example, high energy neutrinos are able to probe at much smaller length scales, so can interact with individual quarks inside the nucleons. In contrast, lower energy ones may interact with the nucleus as a whole \cite{Derek}. This energy dependence leads to there being specific dominant interaction modes at different neutrino energies. For the energies that oscillation experiments are concerned with, there are 3 principal interaction modes that want attention. Figure 2.2 shows at which energies these three different processes dominate.

\subsubsection{Charged-Current Quasi-Elastic (CCQE)}

Considering CC interactions, as these allow for the neutrino flavour to be determined, Figure 2.2 shows that between $10^{-1}$ GeV and around $1$ GeV incident neutrino energy, the most dominant interaction process is that of Quasi-Elastic (QE) scattering. At this energy range, the neutrino will interact at a length scale comparable to the size of a nucleon, and hence the nucleon as a whole. However, the interaction modes are identical for the other two neutrino flavours.} incident on a nucleon of the target nucleus. This interaction mode produces a very clean signal and allows for the incoming neutrino energy to be extracted by the charged lepton kinematics, ie its kinetic energy and its angle with respect to the beam direction \cite{lalakulich}. These are two important variables that will be important in this analysis and will be touched on in more detail in a later section.

Considering now the neutrino energy region between $1$ GeV and around $5$ GeV in Figure 2.2, the dominant interaction process here is so-called Resonant Pion Production (RES). This resonant process is a result of the incident neutrino having enough energy to excite the nucleon producing a baryon-resonance. This resonant state is unstable, so will decay back to a nucleon by emitting a pion. There are many different possible interaction channels for this resonant process. The type of pion ($\pi^0$ or $\pi^{\pm}$) being emitted, as well as the final state nucleon (proton or neutron), will be contingent upon which channel the interaction has undergone \cite{formaggio}. \subsubsection{Deep Inelastic Scattering}
Finally, beyond neutrino energies of approximately $5$ GeV, the Deep Inelastic Scattering (DIS) process is such that the neutrino now has a high enough energy to resolve smaller lengths and therefore will probe inside the nucleons interacting with the constituent quarks. Consequently, the target nucleon will be broken apart, and as a result of colour confinement\footnote{Colour confinement is a principle stating that colour charged particles (quarks and gluons) must be found in colourless bound states, and can never exist as free particles.}, the quarks will hadronise producing a hadronic shower in the detector, from which we can infer DIS. The three interaction modes considered above have each been predicted by separate models. Each of these models are not consistent in their accuracy and hence are incompatible with each other. Therefore, in order to gain a better understanding of the nuclear structure and how it affects its interactions with incident neutrinos, it can be useful to unify these models by studying an energy region where each of these three processes are important. \subsection{Nuclear Effects} \label{sec:NuclearEffects}
When studying neutrino interactions, the charged lepton kinematics\footnote{For this specific analysis, we are interested in looking for $\nu_{\mu}$, so the variables of interest are those arising from the muon kinematics.} and the final state hadronic particles (Section \hyperref[sec:eavail]{4.5}) produced by these interactions are important variables to consider. Needless to say, to understand the hadronic kinematics and the system as a whole, knowledge of the interaction mode is crucial. However, the effect of certain processes that occur inside the nucleus, which might affect the final state hadronic kinematics is a critical consideration. These can make the identification of interaction modes in addition to the reconstruction of these hadronic kinematics far less predictable. To understand these nuclear effects, it is crucial to note that neutrino-nucleus interactions can be split into three categories or steps, namely the motion of the nucleons in the nuclear medium just before the interaction has occurred, followed by correlations of the nucleons during the interaction and finally, the propagation of the hadrons after the interaction. Each of these will be highlighted below.


\subsubsection{The Relativistic Fermi Gas Model}
Firstly, it is important to note that even though the target nucleus is at rest, this does not mean that the neutrino-nucleon interaction is occurring in the lab frame of the experiment. In neutrino oscillation experiments, it needs to be considered that the detectors are composed mainly of hydrocarbon, which make up the plastic scintillators, iron in samplng calorimeters and water in Cherenkov detectors. More on the detector composition follows this in Chapter \hyperref[sec:NOvAChap]{3}. As a result, the target nucleons exist in bound states, forming nuclei. This is in contrast to liquid hydrogen targets where nucleons form the entire nucleus \cite{Cremonesi}. Consequently, it is paramount to acquire a detailed understanding of the nuclear medium in order to make accurate predictions. Since nucleons are spin-$1/2$ particles, they are known as fermions. Therefore, the nuclear medium can be modelled as a so-called relativistic Fermi gas. This is known as the \textit{Relativistic Fermi Gas} (RFG) model \cite{joseph} and in its simplicity, states that the nucleons inside the nucleus, rather than being at rest, are in constant motion at relativistic speeds. Therefore, the neutrino-nucleon interaction will occur in a boosted reference frame rather than the expected lab frame, thereby leading to an incorrect estimation of the final state hadronic kinematics.

\subsubsection{Meson Exchange Currents}
Another effect that has recently been implemented in the latest neutrino interaction generators is that of \textit{Meson Exchange Currents} (MEC). This effect uses the idea that nucleons within the nucleus can form correlated pairs. It is these correlated pairs that the incoming neutrino interacts with. MEC is also known as 2p-2h, since the interaction knocks out two nucleons that can be detected in the final state, leaving two holes in the nucleus. The inclusion of this process in our simulations results in a much better agreement with data as shown in Figure 2.6.

\subsubsection{Final State Interactions} \label{sec:FSI}

\begin{figure}[h!]
\centering
\includegraphics*[scale=0.25]{png/FSI.png}
\caption{Schematic showing the possible final state interactions that can occur inside the nucleus. The final state particles, as they exit the nucleus, may interact in various different ways, leading to altered apparent final states. Diagram taken from \cite{sowjanya}} \label{expansionfigure}
\end{figure}

Finally, we introduce the concept of \textit{Final State Interactions} (FSI). This is the final stage of the interaction process and occurs just prior to detection. After the initial neutrino-nucleon interaction has occurred, the final state particles must exit the nucleus by travelling through the nuclear medium. Consequently, they are at risk of interacting with this nuclear medium, which can lead to altered final state particle kinematic determination and hence misinterpretations of the underlying physics of the interaction. These, along with the other nuclear effects discussed, must be understood and accounted for. Figure 7 shows some examples of FSI types, which include:

\vspace{10pt}

\noindent \textbf{\textit{Charge Exchange: }}This FSI effect concerns the interaction of a final state particle with a nuclear nucleon. A prominent example of charge exchange is the pion-nucleon FSI reaction \cite{golan2}. Put simply, this FSI is one where the final state pion interacts with the nucleon, exchanging charge and exits the nucleus to be detected with a different electric charge. This can easily result in a misinterpretation of the final state particle kinematics and hence the true interaction mode.

\vspace{10pt}

\noindent \textbf{\textit{Elastic Scattering: }}This is quite straightforward in that  the outgoing final state particle simply scatters off a nucleon resulting in the final state kinematics to change, again causing some potential confusion.

\vspace{10pt}

\noindent \textbf{\textit{Absorption: }}Here, the final state particle is completely absorbed by a FSI in the nucleus. The detector will not even see the outgoing particle.

\vspace{10pt}

\noindent \textbf{\textit{Pion Production: }}Finally there is pion production, here the interaction of the final state particle results in an additional pion being produced that can mimic other final state particles and again cause misidentification of the underlying interaction mechanism.

\vspace{10pt}

It is clear that nuclear effects can make some unexpected contributions to the final measurement by altering the final state kinematics in an unpredictable way. Therefore, particle physicists must continue to refine the hypotheses and theories surrounding these effects and incorporate them into the latest models and simulations. By studying neutrino-nucleus interactions as will be done in this dissertation and many other bodies of work, these neutrino event generators can be improved in order to further reduce relevant measurement uncertainties.
